\section{Descartes' \emph{Meditations}}

In Meditation I, Descartes systematically constructs skeptical hypotheses for escalating levels of skepticism.

\begin{enumerate}[label={\arabic*.}]
  \item Ordinary perceptual error cases (local skeptical hypothesis)
  \item Dream hypothesis (global perceptual skeptical hypothesis)
  \item Evil Demon Hypothesis (maximally global skeptical hypothesis)
\end{enumerate}

The Evil Demon Hypothesis is especially powerful because:

\begin{itemize}
  \item It generates \emph{one} skeptical hypothesis for virtually any $P$.
  \item Even mathematical and logical truths become doubtful.
\end{itemize}

Descartes does not immediately reject the Certainty Principle.
Instead, he embraces radical doubt and attempts to rebuild knowledge from a secure foundation (Meditation II onward).

\subsection*{The \emph{Cogito}}

Descartes finds \emph{one} belief that is not subject to doubt via skeptical reflection:

\begin{itemize}
  \item[] \textbf{\emph{Cogito}:} I exist.
\end{itemize}

Why? Two reasons. One particular to the skeptical scenario Descartes is considering, and one general.

\begin{itemize}
  \item[] \textbf{Specific reason:}\\ In the skeptical scenario, the Demon has to deceive \emph{me}, so I must exist.\\[-6pt]
  \item[] \textbf{General reason:} Doubt about one's one existence requires accepting the following:
  \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=4em]
    \item[(D)] ``I doubt that I exist.''
  \end{itemize}
  But accepting (D) implies accepting one's own existence.
\end{itemize}

Also, we know, even if all else is doubted, that we \emph{think}, and it follows from this that we \emph{exist}.
